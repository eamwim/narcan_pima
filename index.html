The Initiative

I am recommending the installation of Narcan (Naloxone) throughout all of Pima Community Colleges' facilities so that this life-saving drug is readily available. Narcan will be placed both in existing emergency cabinets and in stand-alone devices.

As Tucson continues to battle its deadly and ever-changing opioid epidemic, which has been claiming lives in Tucson for over 30 years, residents are seeing the devastating effects of addiction firsthand. Therefore, it is crucial for Tucson to move away from simply responding to emergencies related to the opioid epidemic and begin using harm-reduction strategies proactively.

Making Narcan easily accessible allows students to become empowered to respond to the growing number of people suffering from opioid overdoses. Students, faculty, staff, and visitors will be able to quickly access Narcan without having to search for it, allowing us to develop a rapid, efficient way to help reduce the number of overdoses occurring on college campuses.

Scale and History of Tucson’s Opioid Epidemic

The financial burden of the ongoing crisis is substantial. A recent 2025 Technical Report produced by the Arizona Department of Health Services (ADHS) estimated that the annual cost of opioid-related hospitalizations costs the State of Arizona economy about $2.2 billion per year. Pima County has some of the highest rates of opioid-related hospitalizations in the state.

The Four Waves of the Epidemic

  1. Late 1990s – 2010: Prescription medications were aggressively marketed. Many doctors began treating pain as a "vital sign," creating a large population of chemically-dependent residents in Tucson.
  2. 2010 – 2016: Increased scrutiny on "pill mills" caused physicians to reduce prescriptions, shifting demand towards black-tar heroin. Tucson became a major distribution center, resulting in high casualty counts.
  3. 2016 – 2024: Fentanyl displaced heroin due to lower production and smuggling costs. Local health officials declared a Public Health Emergency.
  4. 2025 – 2026: The streets are saturated with Carfentanil, a synthetic opioid thousands of times stronger than fentanyl. Strategies moved from "law enforcement-first" to aggressive harm-reduction.

Understanding Vulnerable Populations

While there are those who pursue opioids for a high, it is rare that it is solely for that reason. More typically, opioids provide a protective mechanism for the physical discomfort experienced when sleeping on the ground, or serve as a form of self-medication for street-violence-induced trauma.

Bystander Hesitation: A Barrier to Overcoming the Crisis

Deployment strategy is just as important as the medication. Unfortunately, bystander-hesitancy is a major barrier. Typically, bystanders are hesitant due to concerns of medical complications or potential civil/criminal consequences.

However, the CDC notes that Narcan is completely non-threatening to individuals not experiencing an overdose. Furthermore, the Arizona Department of Education strongly supports empowering students to intervene, citing ARS Section 15-341 and ARS section 36-2267, ensuring bystanders are protected under the "Good Samaritan" law. This reflects core healthcare ethics: patient autonomy and allowing patients to protect their communities.

Implementation Strategy

While giving each student a kit may seem like a good solution, it would result in wasted/unused kits. Implementing Narcan through targeted public-access infrastructure is what PCC needs.

Phase 1: AED Blueprints

One of the most immediate and cost-efficient ways PCC can achieve this is by adopting the “proof-of-concept” model utilized by the University of Arizona Health Sciences. By installing Narcan kits inside Automated External Defibrillators (AEDs), PCC eliminates immediate costs for new locations. These require no additional training beyond operating an AED and mirror recommendations to maximize response times to less than three minutes.

Phase 2: Stand-Alone Dispensers

With a safety net of on-campus emergencies provided by AED placement, PCC should deploy stand-alone Narcan dispensers. This bridges campus response and at-home prevention. When we allow students to proactively choose to take medication home (similar to the 2026 STOP-IT Campaign models), we empower them to counteract an overdose in personal environments where it is most likely to happen.
