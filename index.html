The Initiative

I am recommending the installation of Narcan (Naloxone) throughout all of Pima Community Colleges' facilities so that this life-saving drug is readily available. Narcan will be placed both in existing emergency cabinets and in stand-alone devices.

As Tucson continues to battle its deadly and ever-changing opioid epidemic, which has been claiming lives in Tucson for over 30 years, residents are seeing the devastating effects of addiction firsthand. Therefore, it is crucial for Tucson to move away from simply responding to emergencies related to the opioid epidemic and begin using harm-reduction strategies proactively.

Making Narcan easily accessible allows students to become empowered to respond to the growing number of people suffering from opioid overdoses. Students, faculty, staff, and visitors will be able to quickly access Narcan without having to search for it, allowing us to develop a rapid, efficient way to help reduce the number of overdoses occurring on college campuses.

Understanding the Crisis: A Decades-Long Systemic Failure

To understand why this is such a critical issue for Pima Community College, we must look at the systemic failures that created the epidemic in the first place. The crisis our students face today is not an isolated string of personal moral failures, but rather a complex, decades-long public health failure.

The financial burden of this ongoing crisis is staggering. A recent 2025 Technical Report produced by the Arizona Department of Health Services (ADHS) estimated that the annual cost of opioid-related hospitalizations costs the State of Arizona economy about $2.2 billion per year, with Pima County experiencing some of the highest hospitalization rates in the state.

The Four Waves of the Epidemic

Research published in PLOS One outlines how the crisis has evolved through distinct, increasingly deadly "waves" driven by changing supply chains and healthcare policies:

  1. Wave 1: The Prescription Era (1990s): Driven by aggressive pharmaceutical marketing, medical providers began treating pain as a "vital sign." This overprescribing led to widespread chemical dependency across communities like Tucson, planting the seeds for the modern epidemic.
  2. Wave 2: The Heroin Shift (2010): As authorities cracked down on "pill mills" and restricted prescription access, dependent individuals were forced to seek alternatives. Demand shifted toward a massive illicit market for black-tar heroin, turning Tucson into a major distribution hub.
  3. Wave 3: The Synthetic Surge (2013-Present): The illicit market evolved again, replacing heroin with highly potent, incredibly cheap synthetic opioids like fentanyl. Today, synthetics account for 94.5% of overdose deaths in Arizona.
  4. Wave 4: The Pandemic Compound (2020 & Beyond): The compounding effects of the COVID-19 pandemic isolated vulnerable populations and disrupted treatment services. As highly toxic analogs like Carfentanil saturate the streets, local strategies must move from a "law enforcement-first" approach to aggressive harm-reduction.

Understanding Vulnerable Populations

While there are those who pursue opioids for a high, it is rare that it is solely for that reason. More typically, opioids provide a protective mechanism for the physical discomfort experienced when sleeping on the ground, or serve as a form of self-medication for street-violence-induced trauma.

Bystander Hesitation: A Barrier to Overcoming the Crisis

Deployment strategy is just as important as the medication itself. Unfortunately, bystander-hesitancy is a major barrier to saving lives. Typically, bystanders are hesitant due to fears of causing medical complications or facing potential civil/criminal consequences.

However, the CDC notes that Narcan is completely non-threatening to individuals not experiencing an overdose. Furthermore, the Arizona Department of Education strongly supports empowering students to intervene. By acting in good faith, bystanders are entirely protected from professional, civil, and criminal liability under Arizona's "Good Samaritan" law (ARS § 36-2267).

Implementation Strategy

While giving every single student a kit may seem like a good solution, it is financially inefficient and results in wasted/expired kits. Implementing Narcan through targeted public-access infrastructure is what PCC needs to maximize our resources.

Phase 1: AED Blueprints

One of the most immediate and cost-efficient ways PCC can achieve this is by adopting the “proof-of-concept” model utilized by the University of Arizona Health Sciences. By installing Narcan kits inside our existing Automated External Defibrillators (AEDs), PCC eliminates immediate costs for new locations. These require no additional training beyond operating an AED and mirror recommendations to maximize response times to less than three minutes.

Phase 2: Stand-Alone Dispensers

With a safety net of on-campus emergencies provided by AED placement, PCC should deploy stand-alone Narcan dispensers. This bridges campus response and at-home prevention. When we allow students to proactively choose to take medication home (similar to the 2026 STOP-IT Campaign models), we empower them to counteract an overdose in personal environments where it is statistically most likely to happen.
