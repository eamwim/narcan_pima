The Initiative & The Author

Hello! My name is Vincent Duong. I am a student at Pima Community College (PCC) and a proud member of the Phi Theta Kappa international academic honor society. Being born and raised in Tucson, I have witnessed the evolving, destructive impacts of the opioid crisis firsthand. My perspective on this crisis, however, is not merely academic; it is deeply rooted in professional clinical experience. Earning my Certified Nursing Assistant (CNA) credential and stepping into a clinical coordination role at Handmaker has provided me with a frontline view of patient care, medical ethics, and emergency response logistics. In coordinating training and work shifts, I have seen firsthand how critical time, preparation, and immediate medical intervention are to patient outcomes. These clinical experiences have heavily fueled my passion for public health and community safety.

As Tucson continues to battle its deadly and ever-changing epidemic, residents are seeing the devastating effects of addiction firsthand. The financial, emotional, and social toll on our local healthcare systems is immense. Therefore, it is crucial for our community to move away from simply reacting to emergencies and begin proactively instituting harm-reduction strategies. Through this literature review and proposal, I advocate for a realistic, life-saving solution grounded in the core healthcare ethic of patient autonomy: I am recommending the widespread, public installation of Narcan (Naloxone) across all Pima Community College facilities via existing emergency cabinets and stand-alone dispensers. Empowering students to access this medication without barriers is a vital step toward protecting our campus community and saving lives.

Context: A Decades-Long Systemic Failure

To understand why implementing public-access Narcan is a critical imperative for Pima Community College, one must rigorously examine the systemic roots that birthed the opioid epidemic. The crisis our students face today is not merely an isolated string of personal moral failures or individual choices, but a complex, decades-long public health failure. According to the State Health Access Data Assistance Center (SHADAC), a highly credible multidisciplinary health policy research center based at the University of Minnesota, the initial overprescribing of painkillers led to widespread physiological dependency across the nation. This massive wave of addiction eventually shifted toward an enormous illicit market for heroin, and subsequently, highly potent fentanyl due to its extraordinarily cheap chemical production costs, fundamentally altering the public health landscape of the American Southwest (SHADAC, 2026).

The financial and infrastructural burden of this ongoing crisis is staggering. An exhaustive 2025 Technical Report produced by the Arizona Department of Health Services (ADHS) estimated that the annual cost of opioid-related hospitalizations costs the State of Arizona economy approximately $2.2 billion per year. Written by epidemiological experts using extensive statewide data sources such as MEDSIS and death certificates, this report reveals a terrifying reality: the vast majority of opioid overdose deaths (94.5%) were due to synthetic opioids, effectively entirely displacing organic narcotics. Furthermore, Pima County specifically experiences some of the highest rates of opioid-related hospitalizations in the entire state, placing immense strain on local emergency rooms and making localized interventions at large public institutions like PCC incredibly urgent (ADHS, 2025). Importantly, the ADHS report also states that naloxone was successfully administered in greater than 80% of EMS responses statewide, demonstrating its critical, irreplaceable role in immediate survival and the necessity of getting it into the hands of citizens before paramedics arrive.

The Four Waves of the Epidemic

The scale and severity of the current opioid landscape in Tucson did not happen overnight. Rather, as outlined in a highly credible, peer-reviewed academic study hosted by the National Institutes of Health, the crisis has evolved through four distinct, increasingly deadly "waves" driven by shifting global supply chains, regulatory changes, and broader healthcare policies (Laing & Donnelly, 2024).

  1. Wave 1: The Prescription Era (Late 1990s - 2010): The modern epidemic's foundation was laid by aggressive, often deceptive pharmaceutical marketing. During this era, medical providers began treating physical pain as a vital sign, leading to massive overprescribing of drugs like OxyContin. This created a large, chemically dependent population across communities like Tucson, planting the seeds for long-term physiological addiction (SHADAC, 2026).
  2. Wave 2: The Heroin Shift (2010 - 2016): As government and regulatory authorities eventually cracked down on "pill mills" and heavily restricted prescription access, dependent individuals were forced toward illicit markets to avoid severe withdrawal. Demand shifted drastically to black-tar heroin, turning Tucson—due to its geographic proximity to the border—into a major distribution hub. This resulted in unprecedented casualty rates, particularly in mid-town and south-side neighborhoods (Laing & Donnelly, 2024).
  3. Wave 3: The Synthetic Surge (2016 - 2024): The illicit market evolved once again. Because cartels no longer needed to rely on farming vast fields of poppy plants, they pivoted to highly potent, cheaply produced synthetic opioids in underground laboratories. Fentanyl rapidly displaced heroin, prompting local health officials to declare a Public Health Emergency. Synthetics are incredibly lethal because their potency is nearly impossible for street users to measure; today, they account for 94.5% of all opioid-related overdose deaths in Arizona (ADHS, 2025).
  4. Wave 4: Extreme Potency & The Pandemic Compound (2025 - Present): The compounding isolation, economic devastation, and disruption of clinical treatment services caused by the COVID-19 pandemic severely worsened the crisis (Laing & Donnelly, 2024). Currently, the streets are saturated with Carfentanil—a synthetic analog thousands of times stronger than standard fentanyl, originally designed as an elephant tranquilizer. Because of this extreme toxicity, local health strategies have been forced to permanently pivot from a "law enforcement-first" approach to aggressive, community-wide harm-reduction. Survival now depends on immediate chemical reversal, not eventual rehabilitation.

Understanding Vulnerable Populations

Addressing the epidemic on a collegiate level also requires understanding why vulnerable populations utilize these substances, pushing past societal stigmas. While the common narrative assumes individuals pursue opioids strictly for recreational highs, this is scientifically incomplete and harmful to recovery efforts. More typically, opioids provide a protective mechanism to numb the intense physical discomfort experienced by unhoused populations sleeping on the ground, or serve as desperate self-medication for profound, street-violence-induced trauma. Furthermore, the immense economic pressures of modern society push many into chemical coping mechanisms. Acknowledging these complex socio-economic drivers highlights exactly why punitive, criminalizing measures fail, and why accessible medical intervention, like Narcan, is required to keep individuals alive long enough to seek holistic, compassionate rehabilitation.

Review of Alternative Solutions

Prior to arriving at the recommendation for universal, public-access Narcan on college campuses, it is absolutely essential to rigorously evaluate alternative solutions that have been attempted at both the state and local levels. Analyzing the successes, logistical failures, and shortcomings of these prior interventions informs a much more robust and effective strategy tailored specifically for the realities of Pima Community College.

Law Enforcement & Punitive Approaches

Historically, the default response to substance use in Arizona was rooted in a strict "War on Drugs" and "law enforcement-first" paradigm, heavily penalizing possession and use with massive incarceration rates. However, decades of longitudinal data unequivocally prove this approach fundamentally fails to address the root physiological and psychological causes of chemical dependency. Criminalizing addiction directly drives drug use underground, isolating users from safe consumption practices and necessary medical help (Laing & Donnelly, 2024). Crucially, this punitive approach actively increases overdose fatalities, as bystanders and users hesitate to call 911 during an emergency out of a paralyzing fear of arrest or prosecution. Recognizing this catastrophic failure, modern public health policy has aggressively shifted away from punitive measures toward practical harm-reduction strategies that prioritize keeping the patient breathing over making an arrest.

Naloxone Leave-Behind Programs

A recent and much more progressive alternative is the "Naloxone Leave-Behind Program" funded by federal State Opioid Response (SOR) grants. According to an official state government brief from the Arizona Health Care Cost Containment System (AHCCCS), the state utilizes massive federal SOR funding to provide free or heavily subsidized naloxone to community organizations, first responders, and educational institutions (AHCCCS, 2024). Under this specific clinical model, Emergency Medical Services (EMS) personnel are legally authorized to leave a physical kit of Narcan with the family, friends, or roommates of an individual after responding to a non-fatal overdose at a private residence.

While this program is highly effective at preventing secondary overdoses in private homes, this solution is inherently reactionary; it requires an individual to have already suffered an overdose severe enough to warrant a 911 call and an ambulance dispatch. Furthermore, it completely fails to address overdoses that occur in public spaces, highly populated educational institutions, or among individuals who live entirely alone without roommates to hold the medication. While excellent for domestic settings, relying on this model leaves college campuses vastly vulnerable. However, evaluating this alternative is essential to our proposal, as the AHCCCS SOR grant provides a factual, proven framework for how PCC could potentially source the Narcan medication for free, permanently overcoming significant financial objections from the college's Board of Governors (AHCCCS, 2024).

Education-Only Campaigns

Another frequently proposed alternative by hesitant administrators is focusing solely on educational awareness campaigns, hoping that knowledge alone will curb the fatality rate. A rigorous, peer-reviewed study published in the highly prestigious JAMA Pediatrics journal investigated the awareness and knowledge of opioid overdoses specifically among adolescents and young adults. The researchers surveyed thousands of college-aged students to determine their ability to recognize an overdose and their overall willingness to intervene in a medical emergency. The study highlights a vital truth: young adults possess an incredibly high willingness to act and save a life, but critical, fatal gaps exist in their practical knowledge and physical access to lifesaving tools (Freibott et al., 2024).

The conclusion is clear: education alone is entirely insufficient if the physical tools required to save a life are unavailable. A student may perfectly identify the cyanosis (blue lips) and agonal breathing indicative of a severe fentanyl overdose, but without immediate, physical access to an opioid antagonist like Narcan, that theoretical knowledge cannot prevent mortality. Education is vital, but it must be paired with hard, physical infrastructure to be effective.

Strictly Clinical Models

Finally, many educational institutions initially adopted a strictly clinical model out of an abundance of caution, housing Narcan exclusively in locked campus health centers or strictly within the vehicles of campus police. The University of Arizona Health Sciences utilized this exact model before recognizing its massive flaws and pivoting (University of Arizona Health Sciences, 2024). The critical, fatal flaw in a clinical-only model is time. Severe brain damage and brain death from an opioid overdose can occur within a mere three to five minutes due to oxygen deprivation. If a student collapses in a distant library, a remote parking lot, or a sprawling dormitory, the physical transit time required to locate a campus nurse, unlock a clinic, or wait for campus police to be dispatched across campus often far exceeds the viable window for resuscitation. Consequently, widespread public access is mathematically required to maximize survival rates.

The Argument: A Multi-Phase Implementation Strategy

With the current opioid epidemic creating deadlier street conditions than ever before, Pima Community College can no longer afford to wait for proactive solutions. Providing students with the empowerment to intervene directly reflects the primary core principle within healthcare ethics: patient autonomy and empowering citizens to protect their own communities. According to the American College Health Association (ACHA), the leading national organization advocating for the health of college students, institutions of higher education must implement robust public access programs (such as wall-mounted dispensers) in high-traffic academic buildings (ACHA, 2023). Putting Narcan in PCC's public facilities is not a radical, untested local idea, but rather a verified best practice endorsed by the premier national collegiate health authority.

Overcoming Bystander Hesitation: The Psychological Barrier

Deployment strategy is just as important as the medication itself. Unfortunately, bystander-hesitancy remains a major, persistent barrier to saving lives. According to an empirical surveillance report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), nearly 43% of all overdose fatalities occur while a bystander is physically present but fails to act (CDC, 2025). Typically, bystanders hesitate due to severe anxiety regarding causing further medical complications, a lack of confidence, or terrifying concerns regarding potential civil and criminal liabilities if the patient is injured during the intervention.

To implement a successful program, PCC must actively educate students using the CDC's proven safety profile of Narcan. Narcan works by competitively binding to the brain's mu-opioid receptors, effectively kicking the fentanyl off the receptor. However, if there are no opioids present in the system, it does absolutely nothing. The CDC confirms it is completely harmless if administered to someone not experiencing an active opioid overdose, acting effectively like a spray of water (CDC, 2025). Furthermore, the Arizona State Legislature explicitly protects bystanders from lawsuits. Arizona Revised Statutes § 36-2267 outlines the state's robust Good Samaritan protections, definitively stating that any person who acts in good faith to administer an opioid antagonist is entirely immune from professional, civil, and criminal liability (Arizona State Legislature, 2023). Overcoming this psychological hesitation by advertising these legal protections is vital to the success of the physical dispensers.

To establish this physical infrastructure and community readiness, I propose a comprehensive, three-phase implementation plan tailored specifically to the geographic and demographic realities of Pima Community College:

Phase 1: Utilizing AED Blueprints for Immediate Rollout

One of the most immediate and cost-efficient ways PCC can achieve public access is by adopting the highly successful “proof-of-concept” model pioneered by the University of Arizona Health Sciences. This institutional report details their collaborative effort with the Pima County Health Department, which resulted in Narcan kits being seamlessly placed inside 308 existing campus AED cabinets (University of Arizona Health Sciences, 2024). By installing Narcan kits inside Automated External Defibrillator (AED) cabinets, PCC instantly eliminates the massive infrastructural costs, labor, and time associated with acquiring, wiring, and mounting new, specialized boxes. Because AED locations are already strategically mapped by fire marshals across campus to ensure a rapid response, co-locating Narcan perfectly mirrors strict recommendations from the Arizona Department of Education to ensure response times remain under three minutes (Arizona Department of Education, 2025). Furthermore, students already associate AED cabinets with life-saving emergency action, reducing the cognitive load during a panic scenario.

Phase 2: Stand-Alone Dispensers for Take-Home Prevention

Once a foundational safety net of on-campus emergency access is established via AED placement, PCC must aggressively expand the initiative by deploying stand-alone Narcan dispensers in high-traffic, highly visible student hubs (e.g., student unions, cafeterias, and advising centers). These stand-alone dispensers serve a vital dual purpose: they bridge the critical gap between campus emergency response and at-home prevention. Social stigma often prevents students from walking into a clinic and asking a nurse for Narcan. By allowing students to proactively and anonymously take medication home with them from a public dispenser—a methodology strongly supported by the 2025 School Fentanyl and Opioid Response Toolkit (Arizona Department of Education, 2025)—we bypass the stigma. We empower students to counteract overdoses in personal, off-campus environments (like apartments and dorms) where isolation means fatalities are statistically most likely to occur.

Phase 3: Comprehensive Information & Education Campaign

With the recent and highly commendable initiative by the new PCC Chief of Police to begin installing Narcan dispensers alongside existing AED machines, the physical infrastructure of Phase 1 is thankfully already underway. However, physical access without knowledge is useless. Relying heavily on the findings of Freibott et al. (2024) regarding the critical gaps in young adults' practical knowledge, Phase 3 requires launching a massive, campus-wide education campaign. This campaign should extensively utilize digital signage across campus, integrate mandatory 5-minute training modules into freshman orientations, and leverage social media to inform the student body. The curriculum must be simple: indicating that these life-saving tools are readily available, teaching students exactly how to identify the agonal breathing of an overdose, and explicitly advertising that they are protected by the Good Samaritan law when intervening. Educating the community ensures that these new dispensers are utilized effectively and without hesitation during those critical, fleeting first three minutes.
